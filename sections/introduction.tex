\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Elastic Rebound Theory and Earthquake Cycles on Transform Faults}

\begin{itemize} 
    \item In simple cases of stress accumulation and release of seismic faults, earthquakes are expected to occur quasi-periodically, with the recurrence 
    interval determined by the rate of tectonic loading and the amount of slip released during each earthquakes \cite{stress2003}. 
    \item he elastic rebound theory \cite{Reid1910}, together with the seismic gap hypothesis, provides a conceptual framework for understanding the
     accumulation and subsequent release of tectonic strain along fault boundaries.
    \item Shortcommings of the elastic rebound theory in explaining complex earthquake cycles, especially on oceanic transform faults : It neglates the fact 
    that the rupture process may not be uniform along the fault plane, and that the slip distribution can be highly heterogeneous \cite{heaton1990}. 
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Seismotectonic Setting of the Study Area and Fault Structure}

\begin{itemize}


    \item The study area lies along an active plate boundary characterized by quasi-periodic seismicity \cite{Aderhold2016}. The fault has an approximate 
    strike of $98/75/175^\circ$ and accommodates relative plate motion at an average slip rate of approximately $2.24~\mathrm{cm/yr}$ \cite{Aderhold2016,DeMets1990}.

    \item The fault system has traditionally been divided into two major segments, Segment A and Segment B \cite{Searle1981,Whitmarsh1986,Skolotnev2021}.
     Segment A, in particular, shows significant seismicity \cite{Aderhold2016}.

    \item Recent observations of earthquake mechanisms and fault geometry suggest that Segment A can be further divided into two structurally distinct portions: 
    Segment A-East, characterized predominantly by near-vertical strike-slip motion, and Segment A-West, where the fault dips northward and exhibits a component
     of transpression \cite{Janin2026}.

    \item Our particular interest are the 2015 ($M_w$ 7.1) and 2025 ($M_w$ 6.9) earthquake sequences, which occurred along the same fault system within a ten-year interval. Their close 
    temporal and spatial association provides an opportunity to investigate earthquake recurrence, rupture segmentation, and variations in slip distribution along 
    the fault system (Figure~\ref{fig:Charlie_Gibbs_seismicity}).


\end{itemize}

\subsection{Unresolved Questions and Study Objectives}
\begin{itemize}
    \item As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:Charlie_Gibbs_seismicity}, the 2025 earthquake occurred only about 10 years after the 2015 
    earthquake and affected the same section of the Charlie--Gibbs transform fault. Such a short recurrence interval is not evident 
    in the historical seismicity of the fault (Figure~\ref{fig:Charlie_Gibbs_seismicity}(c)). Given the relatively slow plate motion
     of $\sim$2.24~cm/yr \cite{DeMets1990}, only about 22~cm of tectonic displacement would accumulate over 10 years. This is much 
     smaller than the several metres of slip typically associated with a large earthquake. The occurrence of two large earthquakes 
     on the same fault segment within such a short time interval is therefore unexpected in the framework of a simple stress-loading 
     and elastic-rebound model, where a major earthquake is expected to release accumulated strain and consequently delay another 
     large rupture on the same segment.
    \item To investigate: Are the slip distributions of the 2015 and 2025 earthquakes similar or different? If different, what are
     the implications for stress accumulation and release along the fault system?
\end{itemize}
